The Sovereign AI Standard

A public standard for who actually controls the AI you depend on.

Sovereignty is not where the servers are. It is whether you know which model serves you, under whose jurisdiction, trained on what, whether it can change under you, what it runs on, whether you could leave, and whether you can even evaluate it. The Sovereign AI Standard tests all seven — published in full, graded at three levels, and assessed by accredited independent assessors.

§ 01 · What it is

A published specification, and a register you can check.

The Standard exists so that "our AI is sovereign" stops being a claim a vendor asserts and becomes one an independent party can test against written criteria — the same way SOC 2 works for security, and the way an index standard works for classification.

Published

The criteria are public

Every criterion, at every level, on this site — before it is applied to anyone. A rule you can read and argue with is worth more than an assurance you have to take on trust.

Independent

The assessor doesn't own it

The Standard, the mark and the register are held by the scheme, not any assessor — accredited graders cannot change the bar. That separation is the point.

Checkable

A public register

Every certificate — product, level, version, assessor, status — sits on the register, withdrawals alongside grants.

§ 02 · The seven dimensions

Seven ways of asking one question: can you still act?

Can you name it, move it, defend it, steer it, run it elsewhere, leave it, and evaluate it. Each dimension protects one of those options. The full criteria for each are in the standard.

S1

Model sovereignty

Whether you know and can control which model serves you — supply chain to origin, version pinning, change notice, fallback.

S2

Data sovereignty

Where your data is processed, under whose law, and who could compel access — residency, sub-processors, government exposure.

S3

Provenance sovereignty

What the model learned from, and whether you hold clean title to what it produces — corpus rights, output ownership, indemnity.

S4

Operational sovereignty

Whether you can observe, test and refuse change — change notice, evaluation rights, rollback, decision logging.

S5

Infrastructure sovereignty

What compute it rests on and how concentrated that is — hosting topology, provider concentration, customer-controlled deployment.

S6

Exit sovereignty

Whether you could leave, in what timeframe, at what cost — portability, transition assistance, escrow.

S7

Evaluation sovereignty

Whether you can measure it — for capability, bias and reproducibility. Evaluability is the control precondition for EU AI Act compliance; a black box you cannot test has no lawful path into a high-risk use.

§ 03 · Three levels

Disclosed. Controlled. Sovereign.

Certification is graded, not pass/fail — a single bar either excludes almost everyone or certifies almost nothing. Each level is a claim a buyer can act on.

Level 01Disclosed
The buyer can see the whole picture. Every dimension documented and independently verified. Transparency, not yet contractual protection.
Level 02Controlled
The protections are in the contract, not the sales deck. Standard terms carry the commitments that close each dimension, available to any customer.
Level 03Sovereign
The customer could operate independently of any single provider. Demonstrated substitutability, customer-controlled deployment, and an exit that can actually be executed.

§ 04 · How it's run

The Standard is one thing. The assessors are another.

Keeping the rule-maker separate from the graders is what makes the mark worth anything. This is the structure — including the parts still being built.

The scheme owns the standard
The criteria, the mark, the register and the appeals function belong to the Standard's administering body, Sovereign Technology Systems Corporation (B Corp certification pending). Its research arm, Sovereign AI Lab, researches and maintains the criteria. No assessor can add to, waive, or reinterpret a criterion.
Accredited assessors grade against it
Independent assessors are accredited to assess organisations and issue certification under the Standard's rules. A vendor is not locked to one — the Standard admits several, so you can choose your assessor. See assessors →
Built toward external accreditation
The scheme is built to the principles of ISO/IEC 17065 — the international standard for bodies certifying products and services — with the intent that it and its assessors seek formal accreditation once there is operating history to assess. Until then these commitments are self-imposed and public. confirm

§ 02 · Two tracks

The questions change depending on where you sit in the stack.

An application vendor can disclose whose model it uses. A model provider is that model — asking it the same question is meaningless, and asking it to evidence per-item rights over a web-scale corpus is unachievable by anyone. So the standard runs two tracks with different criteria and the same seven dimensions.

Track A

Application layer

Products and services that deliver AI capability built on models the organisation did not train itself — the great majority of enterprise AI software. Criteria test disclosure of the supply chain, contractual protection, and exit.

Who certifies hereSaaS vendors · agents and copilots · vertical AI applications · systems integrators embedding third-party models
Track B — draft

Model providers

Organisations that train, fine-tune, or publish foundation models offered to others. Criteria test lineage, corpus governance, deprecation discipline, and whether a customer can run the model somewhere they control.

Who certifies hereFoundation-model developers · providers of general-purpose AI models · organisations publishing open-weight models commercially

The design constraint that makes Track B possible. No provider can evidence item-level rights across a web-scale training corpus, and a criterion requiring it would certify nobody. Track B therefore assesses provenance at the source-category level, anchored to the disclosure artefacts the EU AI Act already mandates — the public training-content summary and the copyright policy addressing rights reservations. We test that those exist, are current, and are consistent with what we can independently observe. We do not pretend to audit a corpus.

§ 02 · Design rationale

Why the standard has this shape.

A standard that only lists rules invites the question its authors should answer first: why these, at these thresholds? Every criterion in the tables below exists to prevent a specific, observed failure — and the reasoning is published alongside the rules, because a criterion you understand is one you can argue with, and argue for.

Why seven dimensions

One owner, one remedy each

Fewer would blur failure modes that need different fixes; more would fragment without adding a decision. The test for a dimension is whether it maps to a distinct internal owner and a distinct contractual remedy. All seven do.

Why two tracks

The question changes across the stack

Asking an application vendor "whose model is it?" is answerable; asking a model provider the same is meaningless. Separating the tracks is what lets every criterion be both fair and testable for the organisation it applies to.

Why three levels

Pass/fail certifies nobody — or nothing

A single bar either excludes most of the market or sits so low it means nothing. Graded, cumulative levels let a buyer match the requirement to the stakes, and give a vendor a ladder to climb rather than a wall to bounce off.

Why Level 1 is disclosure

The lowest rung has to be reachable

Transparency is achievable by any honest vendor, however the product is built. So Level 1 asks only that you disclose; Level 2 asks the protections be in your standard terms; Level 3 asks you prove independence. Difficulty rises with the size of the claim.

Rationale · the seven dimensions

What each dimension is actually protecting against.

The rationale below applies to both tracks — the criteria differ between application vendors and model providers, the underlying reasoning does not.

S1 · Model sovereignty
You cannot manage a dependency you cannot name. The recurring failure is a buyer discovering, mid-incident, that an "AI feature" was a call to a third-party API on terms they never saw. Disclosure to origin is the precondition for every other protection, and version identification is what makes an incident investigable and a regulator answerable.
S2 · Data sovereignty
Where data is processed decides whose courts can compel it and whose law governs it. This is the dimension the market already calls "sovereignty", and it is non-negotiable in regulated and public-sector contexts. The quiet failure is silent cross-border failover — residency that holds until the primary region doesn't.
S3 · Provenance sovereignty
A model's training data is a liability you inherit without ever seeing it. Unresolved corpus rights surface as third-party claims that standard software indemnities do not reach, and "your data improves our model" quietly redistributes your advantage to competitors. Provenance is where IP risk and competitive risk meet.
S4 · Operational sovereignty
A model that changes under you is a supplier altering your product without telling you. The failure mode is learning of a behaviour change from a customer complaint rather than a release note. The right to be told, to test, and to roll back is the difference between steering a vendor and being steered by one.
S5 · Infrastructure sovereignty
Concentration is invisible one contract at a time and decisive across a whole stack. When many vendors rest on the same model or the same cloud, a single provider event is a correlated outage, not an isolated one. Sovereignty here is knowing the dependency exists and having somewhere else to run.
S6 · Exit sovereignty
Sovereignty you cannot exercise is not sovereignty. If leaving costs more than tolerating a price rise, every renewal is a demand rather than a negotiation. Portability, transition assistance, and — where critical — escrow are what give the six other dimensions teeth, because they put real content behind "or we walk".
S7 · Evaluation sovereignty
You can control an AI and still be unable to prove how it behaves — whether it is accurate, whether it discriminates, whether last week’s result reproduces. Under the EU AI Act a high-risk system must evidence accuracy and robustness (Art 15) and examination for bias (Art 10); a product you cannot evaluate has no lawful path into that use, however capable. This dimension certifies evaluability — the means to reach that verdict — never the verdict itself.

Read down that list and the through-line is deliberate: every dimension is a form of the same question — can you still act? Can you name it, move it, defend it, steer it, run it elsewhere, leave it, and evaluate it. Sovereignty is optionality, and each dimension protects one option.

Twenty-eight Track A criteria: nine required for Level 1, twenty-two for Level 2, all twenty-eight for Level 3. Level 1 is almost entirely disclosure, because transparency is achievable by any honest vendor, and a standard whose lowest rung is unreachable certifies nobody.

Twenty-eight Track B criteria: eleven required at Level 1, twenty-two at Level 2, all twenty-eight at Level 3. Level 1 is again almost entirely disclosure — and note that a provider already meeting its EU general-purpose AI obligations satisfies a meaningful share of it, which is deliberate. We are not asking the market to document itself twice.

Open questions for consultation — Track B

B1.4 and B6.4 are the contested criteria. Requiring weight availability or escrow at Level 3 means that, as drafted, a closed-weight provider cannot reach Level 3 however well it performs on the other twenty-six criteria. That is either the honest consequence of what sovereignty means, or it is a criterion that makes the top level unreachable for most of the market and therefore useless. We hold the first view and are prepared to be argued out of it.

Open-weight models are not automatically sovereign. Publishing weights satisfies B1.4 but says nothing about corpus governance, jurisdiction, or documentation. A permissively licensed model with no training-content summary fails B3.1 at Level 1. We expect this to be unpopular in both directions.

Comment on the draft before it takes effect.

A standard tested in public is stronger than one published without challenge. Where a threshold is wrong, or a criterion unachievable for a legitimate reason, tell us before v1.0 becomes effective.

Governance of the Standard

The rule-maker, the graders, and the referee are not the same party.

A certification mark is worth exactly what its governance is worth. What makes one credible is that the body which writes the criteria is separate from the assessors who apply them and from the function that hears appeals against them. This page sets out that separation — including the parts still being built.

Ask a governance question →How assessors are accredited ↓
Being built in the open

Sovereign AI is a new scheme. The Standard, the mark and the register are held by Sovereign Technology Systems Corporation (B Corp certification pending), separately from any assessor. Its research arm, Sovereign AI Lab, researches and maintains the criteria, and its Research Group hears appeals. Where an operating function is not yet settled — the detail of assessor accreditation and the complaint route — this page says so rather than imply a structure that does not exist. The roles are fixed; the operating detail is being completed.

§ 01 · Who does what

Four roles, held by different parties by design.

The failure mode for any certifier is a single organisation that writes the rules, decides who passes, hears the complaints, and books the revenue. Here those roles are deliberately split.

The Standard — sets the criteria
The criteria, levels and thresholds are owned by the Standard and published before they are applied. No assessor can add to, waive, or reinterpret them for a client. Changes go through the Standard's own consultation. The owning body is Sovereign Technology Systems Corporation; Sovereign AI Lab maintains the criteria.
Accredited assessors — apply them
An accredited assessor tests an organisation against the published criteria on evidence and issues certification under the Standard's rules. The Standard admits several, on common terms, so a vendor is not locked to one grader. See assessors →
The Standard — hears appeals
A determination is appealed to the Sovereign AI Lab Research Group, the Standard's appeal function, which is independent of the assessor that made it. An assessor does not hear appeals against its own decisions — you are not asking the grader to overturn the grader.
The Standard — holds the mark & register
The certification mark SovereignAI (USPTO application Serial No. 99861987, registration pending) and the public register are held by Sovereign Technology Systems Corporation, not by any assessor. A certificate is recorded whichever accredited assessor issued it, and the mark is licensed by the Standard on common terms.
What the mark certifies
The certification statement, as filed: "Artificial Intelligence programs, algorithms, and tools that are architected to maximize the privacy, self-determination, and sovereignty of the user over their data, knowledge, and cognitive models." In the trade, sovereign has a specific meaning: data and analytics that are generated through the use of software are owned and governed by the user, not by the software provider.
§ 02 · Accreditation of assessors

Anyone grading against the Standard is themselves held to it.

An open standard with sloppy assessors is worth nothing — the mark is only as good as the weakest party entitled to grant it. So the Standard admits assessors by accreditation, not by default.

Assessors are accredited, not assumed
An organisation may only assess against the Standard once accredited — competence, method and independence tested before the first certificate, and re-tested periodically. The accreditation path is here. process to confirm
Consistency across assessors
Two accredited assessors applying the Standard to the same evidence should reach the same result. Calibration, shared interpretation notes, and review of borderline determinations exist so the level means the same thing whoever assessed it.
Toward external accreditation
The international standard for bodies certifying products and services is ISO/IEC 17065 — impartiality, competence, consistency, and the handling of appeals and complaints. The scheme is built to its principles, with the intent that it and its assessors seek formal accreditation once there is operating history to assess. Until then these commitments are self-imposed and public. confirm timeline & body
§ 03 · How a decision is challenged

The appeal leaves the assessor entirely.

A determination should be contestable on the record, not relitigated by relationship — and reviewed by someone with no stake in the assessment.

STEP 01

Draft determination

Your assessor records a proposed outcome against each criterion, with evidence and reasons for any shortfall.

STEP 02

Right of reply

You receive the draft and a stated window to correct the record or contest a finding before anything issues.

STEP 03

Determination

The assessor confirms or revises in writing and issues the level the evidence supports under the Standard's rules.

STEP 04

Appeal to the Standard

You may appeal to the Standard's appeal function — independent of the assessor that decided — with written reasons on both sides.

STEP 05

Register record

The outcome is recorded on the public register. Grants, appeals resolved, and withdrawals are all reflected there.

§ 04 · Complaints & surveillance

A mark that is never withdrawn is a mark that is not being watched.

What keeps a certificate honest between assessments is a route for anyone to flag that a holder has slipped, and a scheme willing to act on it independently of the assessor who granted it.

Anyone can raise a concern
A buyer, a competitor, or a member of the public can report to the Standard that a certified product no longer meets the bar. Concerns go to the Standard, not to the assessor that issued the certificate. reporting route to confirm
What a credible concern triggers
A concern that clears triage can open an out-of-cycle review. The holder is shown the concern and given a chance to respond before any action — adversarial-fair, not summary.
Routine surveillance
A lighter check at the twelve-month point tests whether the model supply chain, standard terms, or processing locations have moved. Material change must be notified within a stated window.
Suspension and withdrawal
A holder that no longer meets the bar, fails surveillance, or will not cooperate is suspended and, if unremedied, withdrawn — a decision of the Standard, not the assessor. Both appear on the public register alongside grants.
A check, not a weapon
The mechanism is a check on holders, not a tool for harassing them. Bad-faith or competitively motivated complaints are closed at triage, and a pattern of them is itself recorded.
§ 05 · What every assessor must accept

The impartiality rules the Standard imposes on its graders.

These bind every accredited assessor — not as a promise each makes, but as a condition of accreditation the Standard enforces and can revoke.

No selling the answer
An assessor provides no readiness consulting, remediation advisory, or pre-assessment coaching to an organisation it may assess. You cannot buy help passing from the party grading you.
Non-discriminatory access
An assessor grades any organisation on the same terms and published fee, regardless of size or other relationship. Refusals carry written reasons and a right of appeal to the Standard.
No fees from the supply chain
An assessor takes no revenue or commercial relationship from the model, cloud, or reseller vendors whose products appear in an assessed supply chain.
Declared conflicts
Where an assessor has another relationship with an organisation it assesses, the relationship is disclosed and the work separated — or the assessment is referred to another accredited assessor. The Standard's multi-assessor design exists precisely so that referral is always possible.
Accreditation can be withdrawn
An assessor that breaches these rules loses accreditation, and certificates it issued are reviewed. The rules are conditions, not aspirations.

Hold the scheme to these commitments. They are published to be tested.

Where a governance commitment is vague where it should be specific, or absent where it should exist, tell us. A scheme unwilling to be questioned about its own governance has answered the question.

Positioning summary — for internal circulation
Sovereign AI certification is a voluntary, third-party attestation that a named AI product or model meets published criteria across seven dimensions of customer control: model supply chain, data jurisdiction, provenance and IP, change control, infrastructure dependency, exit, and evaluability. It is NOT a conformity assessment under the EU AI Act, has no regulatory standing, and does not certify accuracy, safety, fairness, or fitness for purpose. It evidences, in substantial part, the downstream-documentation, copyright-policy and training-content-summary obligations applicable to providers of general-purpose AI models in the EU, and partially evidences value-chain information, documentation and logging obligations for AI systems. It does not address risk management, data quality, robustness, human oversight, systemic-risk obligations, or conformity assessment. Existing SOC 2, ISO 27001, ISO 42001 and GDPR processor evidence is accepted where it satisfies a criterion. Criteria are public and versioned; certificates name the version assessed; the register lists withdrawals alongside grants.

§ 02 · Choosing an assessor

Once there is more than one, how to choose.

Because assessment is graded against a single published standard, the result should be the same whoever performs it. What differs is fit — and one hard rule.

The one hard rule — independence from you
An assessor that already advises you, sells to you, or has another material relationship cannot grade you without disclosing it and separating the work — and may have to refer you elsewhere. If you have an existing relationship with an assessor, expect to be referred to another. That is the system working, not failing.
Sector and track fit
Some assessors will specialise — model providers on Track B, regulated-sector applications, particular jurisdictions. Fit shortens the assessment; it does not change the bar.
The result is portable
A Level 2 certificate means the same thing whoever issued it, and moves with you. If you change assessor at renewal, your level and history are the Standard's record, not any assessor's.
Fees are the assessor's; the certificate is the Standard's
Assessors set and publish their own assessment fees and compete on them. The certificate and mark licence are the Standard's, priced separately — so no fee to your grader ever depends on the result.

§ 02 · What an entry means

And what it doesn't.

The certificate is product-scoped
An entry covers the named product, not the organisation. A vendor with one certified product and four uncertified ones appears once. Implying otherwise is a withdrawal condition.
The version is part of the claim
Every entry names the standard version it was assessed against. A v1.0 Level 2 certificate is not the same claim as a v1.2 Level 2 certificate, and the register shows both rather than silently normalising them.
Status is live, not annual
Suspensions appear when they happen, not at the next review cycle. A vendor that fails to notify a material change can be suspended between surveillance points.
It says nothing about accuracy or safety
The standard tests sovereignty — control, provenance, jurisdiction, exit. A certified product is not thereby accurate, unbiased, safe, or fit for your purpose. Those are real questions and this is not the instrument for them.
Absence is not a judgement
A vendor not on the register may simply not have applied. Absence is a gap in evidence, not evidence of a problem — and a buyer should ask rather than assume.

§ 01 · The RFP insert

RFP / RFI requirements insert
AI ASSURANCE REQUIREMENT Where the proposed solution incorporates artificial intelligence, the Supplier shall demonstrate, at the point of response, that it holds Sovereign AI certification at Level 2 or above, or shall provide equivalent evidence addressing each criterion of the published Sovereign AI Standard. Evidence shall address, at minimum: (a) the identity of every model and inference provider in the delivery path, to origin; (b) the jurisdictions in which Customer data will be processed, including failover; (c) the basis on which training, fine-tuning and retrieval corpora were obtained; (d) the notice the Supplier will give before changing any underlying model, and the Customer's right to evaluate that change; (e) the Supplier's commitments on use of Customer data for model training; and (f) the Customer's rights and the Supplier's obligations on exit, including portability and transition assistance. The scored-deficiency line is what changes behaviour. Most questionnaires let a vendor leave a box blank at no cost; scoring silence as a deficiency makes disclosure the cheaper option.

§ 01 · The exposure

The risk transfers to you. The evidence does not.

Undisclosed dependency

Third-party models, unseen terms

An AI feature is frequently a call to a third-party model on terms the buyer never reviewed. When that model changes, fails, or becomes subject to a foreign jurisdiction, the exposure sits with the deploying organisation.

Unpriced silence

The unanswered question carries no cost

Most vendor questionnaires allow a supplier to leave the difficult items blank without penalty. Model origin, data jurisdiction and exit terms commonly go undisclosed until they can no longer be negotiated.

Non-transferable accountability

Obligations you cannot delegate

Under the EU AI Act and sectoral regimes, the deployer retains obligations regardless of who supplied the model. An obligation that cannot be evidenced has not been discharged.

§ 02 · The boundary

The boundary, stated as plainly as the overlap.

Overstated coverage is the fastest way for a standard to lose credibility with the audience that matters. The limits are therefore set out as flatly as the areas of overlap.

It evidences

In whole or substantial part
  • Downstream GPAI documentation, copyright policy and training summary (substantial)
  • Value-chain information, instructions and logging (partial)
  • Model and data supply-chain control across seven dimensions
  • That the product is evaluable — the S7 precondition

It does not

And no assessor may represent otherwise
  • Conformity assessment or CE marking — a regulated process with designated bodies
  • Risk management, human oversight, or systemic-risk obligations
  • Accuracy, safety, fairness or fitness for purpose — the finding, not the means to reach it
  • Any regulatory standing — no regulator has recognised it
§ 03 · Evaluability as a precondition

The dimension most often mistaken for a quality claim.

Evaluation sovereignty certifies the means, not the finding.

The Standard certifies control, not quality. S7 is where that is most easily misread — it is not a mark of a good model. It certifies that the model can be measured: capability, bias, and reproducibility, on a fixed version, on a held-out set.

This matters in law because a product that cannot be evaluated has no lawful path into a high-risk use, however capable. The Standard grades the means to reach a verdict; the assessment of the result remains yours.

A diligence window rarely allows inspection of the model itself. What can be established is whether the target controls it: which model, under whose jurisdiction, trained on what, and whether the business could withstand losing it. The Standard turns that into a graded, evidence-backed signal.

§ 01 · Common exposures in an AI target

Where an AI thesis is most exposed.

Supply chain

Undisclosed model dependency

An AI capability that proves to be a thin layer over a third-party API — on terms the target never controlled, and which surface only after close.

Provenance

Inherited corpus liability

Training-data rights that were never documented, emerging post-close as third-party claims a standard software indemnity does not reach.

Exit

Unpriced lock-in

Switching costs and residency constraints that make each future renewal a demand rather than a negotiation, depressing the multiple being paid.

§ 04 · Applying the Standard in diligence

The benchmark is public; an assessor produces the finding.

The Standard is the measure; an accredited assessor takes the measurement.

The public criteria can be used as a diligence framework directly. Where an independent read is required within a deal timeline, an accredited assessor grades the target against the same seven dimensions and returns findings that convert into deal terms.

Tensor, a founding accredited assessor, applies this instrument to price AI risk in transactions — tensordiligence.com →. Others are being accredited; a certificate is valid whoever issued it.

Optionality is what is being acquired.

Control over the AI a business depends on is a determinant of its value. The public criteria serve as a diligence framework; an accredited assessor can grade a target against them within a deal timeline.
