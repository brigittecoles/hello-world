Classification & benchmarking

Every benchmark is a claim about a peer group.

When we tell you a company's AI lifts gross margin 25% above the median, that number means nothing until you know which median. Most AI benchmarking never answers that question. This is how we answer it — and how we tell you when the answer is weaker than we would like.

Talk to us → See the method ↓
01 // The hidden variable

The peer set is where benchmarks quietly fail.

A diligence finding that a target is "ahead of peers on AI" is only as good as the peer group behind it. Three failures are common, and none of them are visible in the finding itself.

Self-selected comparables. The peer group is chosen after the analysis, and it flatters the conclusion. Change the comparables and the finding reverses.

Marketing categories as measurement categories. "AI-native SaaS" is a useful way to talk about a market. It is not a classification anyone can verify, and it cannot be audited by a counterparty.

Silent small samples. A median computed across four companies is reported with the same confidence as one computed across forty. The reader is never told which they are looking at.

02 // How we classify

Two layers. One recognized standard, one measured fact.

We classify every company we assess on two dimensions. Neither is a Tensor invention you have to take on trust: the first is an external standard you can check, and the second is computed from the assessment's own scores by a fixed rule.

01
The industry spine

GICS — the standard your investors already use

We classify companies using the Global Industry Classification Standard, unmodified — the same framework behind index construction and public-market comparables. Sector, industry group, industry, and sub-industry are recorded as classified, by principal revenue source.

This matters because it is externally verifiable. A banker, an LP, or a buyer's advisor can check our classification against a published standard rather than accepting a category we invented. Where a company's classification is a judgement call rather than a vendor assignment, we record that too — and a company we could not classify definitively is never allowed to influence anyone else's benchmark.

02
The AI archetype

Where AI actually sits in the business

Industry tells you what a company sells. It tells you nothing about where AI sits inside it — and that turns out to matter more for value than industry adjacency does. We assess every company on two AI surfaces: the AI embedded in the product, and the AI used in the company's own operations. The relationship between those two scores determines the archetype.

The archetype is derived, not assigned. It falls out of the scores arithmetically, so it cannot drift with an analyst's impression of a company, and two reviewers cannot reach different answers from the same assessment.

04 // Peer-set discipline

We publish the denominator.

The rules below are fixed in our published standard, not decided engagement by engagement. They exist because the most common way a benchmark misleads is not a wrong number — it is a reasonable number resting on a peer group nobody described.

A floor on sample size
We do not compute a median on fewer than eight companies. Below that, the comparison widens to a broader peer group rather than proceeding on a sample too thin to support it.
A defined widening order
When the tightest peer group is too small, the fallback is prescribed, not improvised — and it deliberately holds the AI archetype constant while widening the industry aperture, because where AI sits in a business predicts its economics better than end-market adjacency does.
Disclosure, every time
Each coefficient carries the peer group it was measured against: the level, the archetype, the vintage, and the number of companies in it. Where we had to widen the comparison, the finding says so in plain language. You should never encounter a benchmark whose basis is broader than it appears.
Conservatism where evidence is thin
Where no admissible peer group exists, the coefficient is held at the median and marked as such rather than estimated. A neutral value we can defend is worth more than a favourable one we cannot.

06 // Sector reads and measurement

How this relates to our sector pages.

Our sector reads — healthcare, software, insurance and financial services, industrials, business services, AI infrastructure, payments — are organized the way buyers and operators actually think about markets, and they carry the failure modes we see repeatedly in each.

They are how we talk about markets. They are deliberately not how we measure them. Measurement runs on the industry standard and the archetype, because those are verifiable and stable, while commercial groupings are neither — they cut across industries, and they change as markets change.

Keeping the two separate is the point. It means our commercial point of view can evolve without quietly moving the benchmark underneath it.

01 // The four commitments

Four rules that hold on every engagement.

These are not negotiated deal by deal. They are how the instrument is built, and every other control on this page follows from them.

01 — Analysis in place

The compute comes to your data

Assessment runs inside a clean room provisioned for the engagement, adjacent to where the evidence already lives. We do not build a copy of the data room on our own infrastructure in order to analyse it.

02 — Never crosses

Raw evidence does not leave the room

What leaves the clean room is the assessment: scores, findings, coefficients, and citations back to the evidence by reference. The underlying documents, telemetry, and extracts stay where they were.

03 — No training

Your data never trains a model

Target data is never used to train, fine-tune, or otherwise improve any model — ours or a vendor's. Benchmarks are built from the standardized scores an assessment produces, never from the source material behind them.

04 — Deleted on close

The room is torn down

On completion the clean room is destroyed, credentials are revoked, and working copies are deleted on a stated schedule. You receive written confirmation of what was deleted and when.

01 // The gap

Three reviews. The model belongs to none of them.

Every enterprise already runs a vendor process. The AI inside the product falls between its lanes — not because anyone is careless, but because no lane was built to hold it.

Owned by InfoSec

Security review

Encryption, access control, incident history, SOC 2. Tests how the vendor protects data. Does not ask whose model is behind the feature, or what it was trained on.

Owned by Legal

Contract review

Liability, term, DPA, SLAs. Redlines the paper in front of it — but the paper rarely mentions model substitution, inference-cost pass-through, or training-corpus indemnity, so there is nothing to redline.

Owned by nobody

The AI itself

Whose model, what it cost, what it learned from, whether it changes under you, and whether you can leave. This is the scope area that decides whether the contract protects you.

This is the same structural gap we find on the buy-side of a transaction — the AI is scoped by no one — pointed at a purchase order instead of a purchase agreement.

02 // The four questions

What actually decides whether this contract is safe.

Every vendor engagement resolves to these four. A vendor who can answer all four in writing is a vendor you can sign with confidently — and the answers belong in the agreement, not in a sales deck.

Whose model is it, really?
A large share of "AI-native" products are a thin wrapper on a third-party foundation model. That is not disqualifying — but it means your service depends on a provider you have no contract with, whose pricing, availability, and deprecation schedule you cannot see. We map the model supply chain to its origin and tell you which links you are exposed to.
What happens to your price when inference cost moves?
Someone else's model means someone else's cost curve. If the vendor's gross margin depends on inference cost falling, and it doesn't, that pressure arrives at your renewal. We test whether the pricing you are being offered is durable or subsidised.
What was it trained on, and what do you inherit?
Unresolved training-data rights are a third-party claim waiting to happen, and standard indemnity language frequently does not cover it. We establish the provenance the vendor can actually evidence — and where they cannot, that becomes an indemnity you ask for rather than a risk you absorb silently.
Can you leave?
Models get deprecated, behaviour drifts, vendors get acquired. If your workflow, your prompts, and your accumulated data are not portable, the renewal is not a negotiation. We score exit cost before you are subject to it.

A

Model supply chain

5 findings
A1
Sole dependence on a third-party foundation model, with no fallback
Exposure — The provider deprecates the model, reprices it, or rate-limits it. Your service degrades and you have no contract with the party that caused it.
Clause — Model-continuity and substitution terms: advance written notice of any change of underlying model, an equivalence standard the substitute must meet, and a remedy if it does not.
Illustrative language
"Supplier shall give Customer no less than ninety (90) days' written notice before replacing, retiring, or materially altering any Underlying Model used to deliver the Services. Any replacement shall perform at or above the Performance Standard measured on the Evaluation Set. Where it does not, Customer may (a) require remediation within thirty (30) days, or (b) terminate the affected Services without penalty and receive a pro-rata refund."
A2
The model supply chain is undisclosed or misrepresented
Exposure — You believe you are buying proprietary technology and are in fact buying an integration. Your diligence, your board reporting, and your own customer commitments all rest on a false premise.
Clause — A disclosure warranty naming every model and inference provider in the delivery path, with notification on change and a right to terminate for material misstatement.
A3
Sub-processors and inference regions are not disclosed
Exposure — Your data is processed in jurisdictions you have not assessed, by parties you have not approved, under terms you have not seen.
Clause — A named sub-processor schedule with processing locations, a right of objection to additions, and residency commitments where required.
A4
No contractual flow-down from the vendor's own model provider
Exposure — The vendor's upstream terms give them less protection than they are promising you. Their commitments to you exceed what they can actually deliver.
Clause — A flow-down warranty: the vendor warrants its upstream terms are sufficient to meet its obligations to you, and indemnifies the gap where they are not.
A5
Model version in production is undocumented and unpinned
Exposure — You cannot reproduce a result, investigate a complaint, or evidence to a regulator which model made a given decision.
Clause — Version identification and record-keeping: the vendor logs which model version served each request and retains those records for an agreed period.
B

Cost & pricing durability

5 findings
B1
Gross margin depends on inference cost continuing to fall
Exposure — The price you are quoted is subsidised by an assumption. When it fails, the correction arrives at your renewal as an increase you did not budget for.
Clause — Multi-year price protection with a stated cap on any increase, and an explicit cap on cost pass-through however it is characterised.
Illustrative language
"Fees shall not increase during the Initial Term. On each renewal, any increase shall not exceed the lesser of (a) three percent (3%), or (b) the increase in [named index] over the preceding twelve months. Supplier shall not levy any surcharge, usage adjustment, or pass-through charge referable to its own costs of inference, compute, or model licensing."
B2
Pricing is metered on a unit the vendor controls
Exposure — Billing is denominated in tokens, credits, or "actions" whose definition the vendor can change. Your cost rises without your usage changing.
Clause — A fixed definition of the billing unit for the term, with a commitment that model or architecture changes will not increase units consumed for equivalent work.
B3
No committed-use discount or overage protection
Exposure — Adoption succeeds and the bill scales faster than the value. Internal enthusiasm becomes a budget problem.
Clause — Tiered pricing fixed for the term, an overage rate no higher than the committed rate, and a spend cap with notification before it is reached.
B4
Free or heavily discounted pilot with no priced production path
Exposure — You build a dependency at pilot economics and discover production economics after the workflow is embedded and switching is expensive.
Clause — Production pricing agreed and binding at the point the pilot starts, with a maximum uplift from pilot to production rates.
B5
Vendor's funding runway is short relative to your contract term
Exposure — A distressed vendor raises prices, cuts support, or is acquired mid-term. Your continuity risk sits on their balance sheet, not yours.
Clause — Change-of-control notification with a termination right, plus escrow or continuity provisions proportionate to how critical the service is.

Data rights & IP

5 findings
C1
Training-corpus rights unresolved or undocumented
Exposure — A third-party claim against the model becomes your problem too. Standard software IP indemnities frequently do not reach training data.
Clause — An IP indemnity that expressly covers claims arising from the training, fine-tuning, or retrieval corpora — not only the software — and is not capped at fees paid.
Illustrative language
"Supplier shall defend, indemnify, and hold harmless Customer against any claim that the Services, any Underlying Model, or the data used to train, fine-tune, or ground any Underlying Model infringes or misappropriates the intellectual property rights of a third party. This indemnity shall not be subject to the limitation of liability set out in clause [x]."
C2
Your data is used to train or improve the vendor's model
Exposure — Your proprietary material improves a product your competitors also license. The advantage you paid for is redistributed.
Clause — An explicit no-training commitment covering inputs, outputs, and metadata, with retention limits and deletion on termination.
Illustrative language
"Supplier shall not use Customer Data, including prompts, inputs, outputs, embeddings, or usage metadata, to train, fine-tune, evaluate, or otherwise improve any model, service, or dataset made available to any other person. Supplier shall delete all Customer Data within thirty (30) days of termination and certify deletion in writing."
C3
Ownership of generated output is silent or reserved to the vendor
Exposure — You cannot establish ownership of work product your business now depends on, and cannot pass warranties down to your own customers.
Clause — Output ownership assigned to you, or a perpetual, irrevocable, sublicensable licence surviving termination, with no residual vendor claim.
C4
No warranty that outputs are free of third-party rights
Exposure — A generated asset reproduces protected material and you publish it. The exposure is yours and the vendor disclaims it.
Clause — An output-level warranty and indemnity, or at minimum a documented filtering and provenance control you can rely on and evidence.
C5
Retrieval corpus mixes your data with other customers' or public sources
Exposure — Cross-tenant leakage, or answers grounded in sources you did not license and cannot audit.
Clause — Tenant isolation warranty for retrieval and embedding stores, with disclosure of any shared or public grounding sources.
D

Change control & performance

5 findings
D1
No notification of model, prompt, or system-message changes
Exposure — Output behaviour shifts silently and breaks a workflow you built on it. You discover it from a user complaint, not a release note.
Clause — Material-change notice, plus the right to test any change against your own evaluation set before it reaches production.
Illustrative language
"Supplier shall notify Customer in writing at least thirty (30) days before deploying any change reasonably likely to materially alter the content, format, or accuracy of Outputs. Customer may evaluate such change in a non-production environment against the Evaluation Set, and may defer deployment to its tenant for up to sixty (60) days where results fall below the Performance Standard."
D2
No evaluation or monitoring evidence exists
Exposure — Accuracy risk sits with you by default and you have no baseline from which to argue that performance has degraded.
Clause — A performance commitment measured on a defined evaluation set, reported periodically, with service credits for sustained shortfall.
D3
Accuracy claims are unbenchmarked or benchmarked by the vendor alone

The scheme's own governance — who owns the standard, how assessors are accredited, how a determination is appealed, and how complaints and surveillance work — is published and maintained by the Sovereign AI Standard, independently of any assessor. 01 // Four roles, one held by Tensor

Tensor grades. The Standard does everything else.

The failure mode for any certifier is a single organisation that writes the rules, decides who passes, hears the complaints, and books the revenue. Those roles sit with different parties by design — and the one Tensor performs is deliberately the narrowest.

The Standard — sets the criteria
The criteria, levels and thresholds are owned by the Standard and published before they are applied. No assessor can add to, waive, or reinterpret them for a client. Read the standard ↗
Tensor — applies them
An accredited assessor tests an organisation against the published criteria on evidence and issues a determination under the Standard's rules. This is the one role Tensor holds. Its job is to apply the standard as written — not to shape it, and not to grade generously for a client or harshly for anyone else.
The Standard — accredits assessors & hears appeals
Assessors are admitted by accreditation, not by default, and a determination is appealed to the Standard's own appeal function — independent of the assessor that made it. You are never asking the grader to overturn the grader. How assessors are accredited ↗
The Standard — holds the mark, register & complaints
The certification mark, the public register, and the route to report a slipped holder all belong to the Standard, on common terms across every assessor. The scheme's governance, appeals and surveillance ↗

Read down that list and Tensor appears once. Everything that would let a grader mark its own homework — owning the rules, accrediting the graders, hearing the appeals, controlling the mark — sits with the Standard instead. That is the structural version of independence, not the promised kind.

02 // Tensor's independence as an assessor

The rules Tensor accepts so its assessments can be trusted.

Separating the Standard from the assessor removes most of the conflict. These are the rules that close the rest — the ones specific to Tensor being both a diligence firm and an accredited assessor. Some cost real money, which is how you know they are doing work.

Tensor does not sell the answer
Tensor provides no readiness consulting, remediation advisory, or pre-assessment coaching to any organisation it may assess. You cannot buy help passing from the party grading you. The criteria are the Standard's and published in full, so no interpretation needs to be bought from anyone.
Non-discriminatory access
Tensor assesses any organisation on the same terms and at the same published fee, regardless of size or of any other relationship with Tensor. And because the Standard admits other assessors, an organisation that would rather not be assessed by Tensor has somewhere else to go.
No fees from the supply chain
Tensor takes no revenue, referral, or commercial relationship from the model providers, cloud providers, or resellers whose products appear in an assessed supply chain.
Firewalled from Tensor's other work
Where an organisation Tensor assesses also appears in a Tensor buy-side, vendor, or transaction engagement, the relationship is disclosed to the client and the teams are separated. Where that separation cannot be achieved, Tensor declines the engagement — or refers the assessment to another accredited assessor — rather than compromise either.
The revenue Tensor gave up
To assess cleanly, Tensor has exited vendor-side advisory — the business of helping vendors prepare. It is a line it could otherwise sell into the same relationships. That is the trade the trust is made of.
